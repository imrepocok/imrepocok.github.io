Corolla —
Driving Habits & the EV Decision

Four years of real telematics from my own Toyota Corolla Hybrid, turned into an honest answer to a question every driver faces: is replacing a paid-off, already-efficient hybrid with an electric car actually worth it — and if the case isn't financial, what is it?

Built end to end with a modern analytics-engineering stack — dbt + DuckDB + Python — my first project on these tools.

The question

Switching to an EV is tempting, but the honest test isn't "is electricity cheaper than petrol per kilometre" — it is. The real question is whether replacing a paid-off, efficient hybrid pays off at all, and at what cost. So instead of brochure figures, I costed every one of my real trips under each candidate car, using my actual measured fuel use for the hybrid and community real-world figures for the alternatives.

The headline metric isn't payback in years — it's euros paid per tonne of CO₂ avoided. That reframes an EV from a financial decision into an environmental one, and lets you compare it against what carbon actually costs.

The verdict — an expensive way to cut carbon

On four years of real driving, every EV avoids CO₂ at €5,400–5,800 per tonne. For context, carbon trades at roughly €80/tonne on the EU market — so replacing a working hybrid is something like 65–70× more expensive than the market price of the emissions it saves. That's not an anti-EV result; it's the honest one. The financial case doesn't close, so the decision rests on commitment, not the fuel bill.

The efficiency ladder

Before electric even enters the picture, the hybrid already sits well below a conventional petrol car on running cost. The ladder runs petrol → hybrid → electric — each step cheaper to run per 100 km. The point is that the hybrid is already an efficient starting point, which is exactly why the EV's extra purchase cost is so hard to justify on money alone.

Running cost — € per 100 km, real-world

The classic question — does the premium pay back?

At the dealership, the first question is always whether the pricier car earns its premium back. Run on real driving, the answer splits sharply. The hybrid's roughly €2,500 premium over the conventional 1.2 Turbo petrol is repaid in about 7 years of fuel savings — comfortably within the car's working life. The EVs would take on the order of 80 years to recoup their far larger premium on fuel alone. One is an investment that pays back; the other only makes sense on grounds other than money.

How a hybrid really drives

With measured highway distance for every trip, the fuel split reveals the counter-intuitive truth about hybrids: they're thirstiest on the highway (steady high speed, little regeneration) and most efficient in town — the opposite of a conventional car. My driving is 59% highway, right where the hybrid is weakest, and it still averages around 5 l/100 km. That's the real strength of the car, visible only once the data is split by road type.

Fuel consumption by road type — l/100 km, measured

The hard part — filling a data gap, and proving it

The car only recorded highway distance until its data feed stopped in 2024. Rather than drop the post-2024 trips, I routed every trip's fastest path through the OpenRouteService API, measured the motorway portion, and calibrated it against the years where the car's own measurement existed. A single factor of 0.893 aligns the routed estimate to the measured truth.

Crucially, I didn't just impute the gap — I validated the imputation against ground truth. Year by year, the calibrated estimate lands within ~2–3% of what the car actually recorded, so the same method can be trusted for the years the car never measured.

Where the car goes

Every trip mapped as a heatmap — recurring corridors (Berlin ↔ family in Slovakia and Hungary) burn darkest, and the holiday-season cross-border pattern is visible at a glance. Coordinates are deliberately offset for privacy.

How it was built

A full analytics-engineering pipeline, all local and free. Python scripts prepare the raw telematics and enrich each trip (per-trip temperature from the Open-Meteo archive, routed highway from OpenRouteService). dbt models the data in layers — staging → intermediate → marts — on a local DuckDB warehouse, with tests on every key column. The published site is static, generated straight from the marts.

The core idea is a single fact table costing every trip under every car and every consumption source (lab, community real-world, and my own measured driving), so all the comparisons downstream — the ladder, the green premium, the road-type split — fall out of one consistent model.

dbt model · highway calibration factor ▾ 
-- one number that aligns routed motorway to the car's measured highway,
-- computed only on the months where both exist (the overlap)
with overlap as (
    select
        sum(measured_hw_km) as measured,
        sum(routed_hw_km)   as routed
    from {{ ref('mart_highway_calibration') }}
    where measured_hw_km is not null
)
select
    round(measured / nullif(routed, 0), 3) as calibration_factor
from overlap
